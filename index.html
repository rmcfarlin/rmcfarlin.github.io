Operating Principles

Non-negotiables for high-stakes capital decisions.

These are not aspirations. They are the constraints under which the best long-horizon decisions are made.

01
Relentlessly pursue purposeful progress

Motion without direction is noise. Every system, report, or automation must earn its place by moving the business forward in a way that can be measured and defended at any time horizon.

02
Give more in value than you receive in compensation

Trust compounds faster than capital. Deliver clarity early, surface problems before they become expensive, and make recommendations that serve the whole enterprise across multiple decades.

03
Everyone sweeps the floor

No task is beneath the person accountable for the outcome. Titles are temporary. Standards are not. The leader who will not do the work cannot judge the work.

04
Share good news quickly, and bad news even quicker

Surprises are the most expensive input in any system. The earlier the signal reaches those who can act, the smaller the correction required. This is respect for capital and for people.

05
Treat every experience as an opportunity to learn

The best operators remain students. Whether the variance is favorable or unfavorable, the only useful question is: what did we see too late, and how do we see it sooner next time?

Long Bets

The disciplines that scale to civilizational problems.

The same first-principles thinking required to run a multi-plant manufacturing company with 3-day closes applies to the largest, longest, hardest capital allocation problems humanity will face this century.

Artificial intelligence as leverage

AI is not a replacement for financial discipline. It is the ultimate sensor and actuator for it. The opportunity is to collapse the latency between signal and decision: variance surfaced while it is still small, forecasts that update with live operating data, and the removal of manual drudgery so that human judgment is spent only where it compounds.

The CFO who treats AI as a force multiplier for existing controls and models will outpace the one waiting for perfect data or perfect tools.

Space and the hardest capital problems

Space is the longest-duration, highest-uncertainty capital allocation problem we have ever attempted at scale. The principles are identical to plant-level investment, only the consequences of being wrong are larger and the time constants are measured in decades. Patient capital, staged commitments tied to physics and milestones, ruthless revision when the data changes.

The organizations that will still be relevant in 2050 are the ones applying this rigor today — only harder.
