# Liquidity Is a System

The old line is right: profit is an opinion, cash is a fact. The income statement is a set of judgments — about revenue timing, about reserves, about useful lives. The bank balance is not a judgment. It cleared or it didn't.

But most companies manage cash the way they manage the weather: they observe it. The balance is checked, the line is drawn on, month-end is survived. Liquidity deserves better than observation. It is a system — with inputs, latencies, buffers, and controls — and it can be engineered like one.

Companies don't fail from losses. Plenty of businesses lose money for years and live. Companies fail from running out of money and time at the same moment. The purpose of the liquidity system is to make sure those two never run out together.

## The 13-week forecast is the most honest document in the company

The core instrument is the 13-week cash flow forecast. It came out of the restructuring world, where it is the first document a turnaround advisor builds, and its logic is simple: thirteen weeks is a quarter — long enough to act on what you see, short enough that you can't hide behind assumptions.

It works because of three properties the monthly budget doesn't have.

**It is direct.** Receipts and disbursements, by week, by name. Not "revenue less expenses plus depreciation" — actual checks in, actual checks out. Customer X pays in week 3. Payroll hits every other Friday. The tooling invoice lands in week 9. Accrual accounting is how we measure performance; cash accounting is how we survive. The 13-week speaks only the second language.

**It is falsifiable weekly.** Every week, last week's forecast meets last week's actuals, and the variance is investigated. The forecast gets sharper because it keeps getting graded. A budget is wrong once a year; a 13-week forecast is wrong every week, in public, which is exactly why it improves.

**It forces specificity.** You cannot put "improve collections" in a weekly cash forecast. You can only put "collect the $400K from the customer whose invoice has been disputed for three weeks." The instrument converts intentions into names and dates.

Healthy companies skip this discipline because they can. That is backwards. The best time to install the cash system is when you don't need it — the same logic as everything else in this essay.

## The cheapest capital is already inside the building

PwC's global working capital study puts €1.56 trillion of excess working capital on corporate balance sheets worldwide, with days sales outstanding up 6.6% over five years ([PwC, *Working Capital Study 24/25*](https://www.pwc.co.uk/services/value-creation/insights/working-capital-study.html)). That is not an abstraction. It is cash companies already earned, sitting in receivables they haven't collected and inventory they didn't need to build — while many of the same companies pay elevated rates to borrow.

The cheapest capital you will ever raise is trapped in your own working capital. No covenants, no fees, no dilution. In manufacturing, it hides in three places:

**Inventory is where cash goes to sleep.** Raw material bought ahead of need because the buyer got a volume price. WIP swollen by long changeovers and batch sizes nobody has challenged since the machine was installed. Finished goods built to smooth the schedule rather than to ship. Every one of those is a decision that converted cash into comfort, and most were made without anyone pricing the cash. Inventory age belongs on the plant dashboard next to scrap — visible weekly, owned by name, priced at the cost of capital.

**Receivables problems are usually invoice problems.** The instinct is to blame collections. But trace late payments to root cause and a large share start as disputes — wrong quantity, wrong price, missing PO reference, paperwork that doesn't match the receipt. The customer isn't slow; the invoice is broken. Invoice accuracy is a working capital lever that costs nothing and belongs to you entirely.

**Payables are strategy, not stretching.** Paying late by policy torches supplier goodwill you will want during the next shortage. The move is to negotiate terms deliberately — and then pay exactly on them. Predictability is worth more to a supplier than speed, and it is worth negotiating for.

On long-cycle work — tooling programs especially — progress billing converts months of your cash into your customer's deposit schedule. If you build tooling and aren't billing at milestones, you are your customer's bank, at a rate of zero.

## Raise capacity before you need it

The third layer of the system is credit capacity, and the rule is the same as the forecast's: build it when you don't need it.

I ran this play as a controller. We took our primary banking relationship to a competitive, multi-lender process — not because we were in trouble, but precisely because we weren't. The result was a revolving credit facility three times the size of the previous one, at a borrowing cost 50 basis points lower. Both improvements came from the same source: negotiating from strength, with time on our side and options at the table.

The worst time to raise capacity is when you need it. Tight covenant, soft quarter, nervous lender — every term moves against you, and the clock does too. Unused capacity, by contrast, is cheap insurance against the real killer, which is not the crisis itself but being forced to make large decisions quickly. Distress pricing exists in every market; the discount is for sellers who ran out of time.

Capacity is how you buy time in advance.

## The system, assembled

Put the three layers together and liquidity stops being a number you observe and becomes a system you run:

The **13-week forecast** is the sensor — it tells you what's coming while you can still act. **Working capital discipline** is the pump — it recovers the cash your own operation traps. **Credit capacity** is the reservoir — sized before the drought, priced from strength.

And underneath all three, the same foundation as everything else in modern finance: the forecast is only as good as the data feeding it. When receivables aging, open POs, production schedules, and payroll calendars live in one governed data layer instead of six spreadsheets, the 13-week stops being a Friday-afternoon assembly project and becomes something close to continuous.

Liquidity buys time. Time is what turns a crisis into a decision. The whole system exists so that when something breaks — a customer fails, a machine dies, a market turns — you are choosing among options instead of accepting terms.
