# Profit Concentrates

The income statement tells you the company made money. It will not tell you where. Averages are where the truth goes to hide, and a consolidated P&L is the biggest average in the building.

Ask a different question — *which customers, which parts, which machines actually generate the profit?* — and the answer, in almost every manufacturing business ever measured, has the same uncomfortable shape.

## The whale curve

Rank your customers from most profitable to least. Plot cumulative profit as you go down the list. The line rises steeply, flattens, then falls. It looks like a whale coming out of the water, and the research behind it comes from Robert Kaplan and Robin Cooper's activity-based costing work at Harvard Business School.

The consistent finding: the most profitable 20% of customers typically generate between 150% and 300% of total profits. The middle 60–70% roughly break even. The least profitable 10–20% destroy between 50% and 200% of profits — pulling the company back down to the 100% it reports ([Baker Tilly, *Visualizing customer profitability with the whale curve*](https://www.bakertilly.com/insights/visualizing-customer-profitability-with-the-whale-curve); see also [Customer Profitability Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customer_Profitability_Analysis)).

Read that again with your own ledger in mind. If the curve holds — and it usually does — your best customers are subsidizing your worst, and the P&L is structurally incapable of showing you.

The 80/20 rule undersells it. Pareto says 80% of profit comes from 20% of customers. The whale curve says more than *all* of it does.

## Why standard costing hides the whale

Manufacturing accounting was built to value inventory, not to locate profit. Overhead gets absorbed on machine hours or labor dollars, which spreads the cost of chaos evenly across everything — like peanut butter.

But chaos is not evenly distributed. Setups cluster on the short-run parts. Expedited freight clusters on the customer who forecasts nothing and panics monthly. Engineering time clusters on the legacy program with the drawing that hasn't been current since the second revision. Quality escapes, extra inspections, special packaging, thirty-minute changeovers for twenty-minute runs — all of it lands on specific parts and specific customers, and standard costing bills it to everyone equally.

The result: the messy low-volume account looks acceptable and the clean high-volume account looks merely good. The costing system is quietly taxing your best business to flatter your worst.

## You don't need perfect ABC

Kaplan-era activity-based costing earned a reputation for dying under its own weight — armies of analysts interviewing supervisors about time allocation, models outdated by the time they were built. That reputation is deserved and also no longer relevant.

You do not need to allocate everything. You need honest treatment of the four or five distortions that matter: setups and changeovers, freight (especially expedited), engineering and quality time, order and invoice frequency, and inventory carrying by part. Get those roughly right and the whale surfaces on its own. Precision beyond that changes no decisions.

What has changed since the 1990s is the cost of getting the data. When production, labor, shipping, and financial signals land in one lakehouse with governed definitions, cost-to-serve stops being a consulting project and becomes a query. The analysis Kaplan needed a team for now runs continuously in the background. The barrier today is not the math. It is the willingness to look.

## What to do with the tails

The naive reading of the whale curve is "fire the bottom customers." That is usually wrong, and it skips the sequence. The bottom of the curve is not a list of villains — it is a list of mispriced relationships.

Work the sequence:

**Price the chaos.** The customer who orders in panic quantities with weekly schedule changes is buying flexibility — a real product with a real cost. Charge for it: expedite fees, minimum runs, change-order pricing. Some customers will fix their behavior. Others will pay. Both outcomes move them up the curve.

**Fix the cost-to-serve.** Some losses are self-inflicted: invoice errors that trigger disputes, changeovers that take three times what they should, packaging nobody re-examined in a decade. The customer isn't unprofitable; the process is.

**Rebalance the mix.** Quote new work like the profitable work you already have, not like the work you're trying to escape. The whale curve is a quoting discipline as much as a pruning discipline.

**Then, and only then, walk away** — knowingly, from relationships that stay value-destroying after repricing and process fixes. This is a small minority. But carrying them unknowingly is how a plant runs full and earns nothing.

And protect the head of the whale. The 20% generating 150–300% of your profit deserve your capacity in a constraint, your best service levels, and your attention before anything at the tail gets a minute of it. Losing one of them costs more than firing ten from the bottom.

## The discipline generalizes

Customers are just the first place to point the lens. The same concentration shows up in parts, in plants, in machines, in workflows. A handful of SKUs drive most of the margin. One constraint governs the throughput of an entire operation. A few workflows consume most of the finance department's hours. The 80/20 question — *where do the few live that drive the most?* — is the highest-yield question in operating finance, and it can be asked of anything with a ledger behind it.

The consolidated number is what you report. The distribution is what you manage. You cannot manage an average — so find the tails, and put your effort where the concentration is.
